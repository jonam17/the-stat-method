The idea behind progressive overload is old and simple: a muscle adapts to what you ask of it, so
to keep adapting you have to keep asking a little more. Where people go wrong is in assuming that
"more" means one thing — a heavier bar, every session, forever.

The evidence describes something broader and more forgiving. You can progress by adding weight,
but also by adding reps, and for muscle growth the two work about equally well. Knowing which to
do next, and when the load should finally go up, is most of the skill.

<h2 id="what-it-means">What progressive overload means</h2>

The American College of Sports Medicine's 2009 position stand defines progressive overload as the
gradual increase of stress placed on the body during training, and lists several ways to apply it:
a heavier load, more total repetitions at the current load, changes to repetition speed, changes to
rest periods, and more total volume. [1] Heavier weight is one lever among several.

That matters because adding weight is not always available. Dumbbells jump in fixed steps, cable
stacks in coarse ones, and on small exercises even the smallest step can be a large fraction of
the load. Adding reps is always available — and, as the next section shows, it counts.

Adding sets is another lever, and the one with the most direct evidence behind it. Our
[guide to training volume](/articles/training-volume-how-many-sets-do-you-actually-need/) covers
how many sets that evidence supports; this article is about the session-to-session decision of
what to change next.

<span class="summary-label">Summary</span>
<div class="summary-body"><p>Progressive overload is the gradual increase of what you ask of a muscle. Heavier weight is one way to apply it; more reps at the same load, more sets, and changes to how the reps are done are others.</p></div>

<h2 id="reps-count">Adding reps is progress</h2>

The most direct test of this is a 2022 trial that randomised 43 resistance-trained adults — 27 men
and 16 women, averaging 23 years old — to one of two ways of progressing the same lower-body
programme for eight weeks. [2] One group kept the rep range at 8–12 and added load whenever they
could; the other kept the starting load and added reps. Both trained twice a week, with sets taken
to or close to failure.

Muscle growth was similar between the groups at nearly every site measured, and the one muscle
that differed slightly favoured the reps group. [2] Squat strength rose by roughly 20 kg in both;
the load group's gain was about 2 kg larger, but the uncertainty ran from about 8 kg in its favour
to about 2 kg against, so the authors judged the difference of questionable practical importance.
Their conclusion was that both progressions are viable strategies.

The study has the limits the authors list: young lifters, lower-body muscles only, eight weeks,
and a strength test on a Smith machine after training with a free-weight squat. [2] It is one
trial, not a settled answer. But it is the cleanest evidence available that a lifter adding reps
at a fixed weight is progressing, not standing still.

<aside class="summary">
<span class="summary-label">Summary</span>
<div class="summary-body"><p>In a randomised trial of trained lifters, progressing reps at the same load built about as much muscle and strength over eight weeks as progressing the load. One short trial in young adults, but direct evidence that reps count.</p></div>
</aside>

<h2 id="when-load-matters">When the load has to rise</h2>

If reps work, why add weight at all? Because muscle size and strength respond to load
differently.

A 2021 network meta-analysis pooled 28 studies and 747 healthy adults, all training with sets
taken to failure, and compared light loads (more than 15 reps per set), moderate loads (9–15) and
heavy loads (8 or fewer). [3] Muscle growth did not differ between them. Strength did: heavy and
moderate loads both beat light loads, and heavy loads edged moderate ones, though that last
difference was not statistically significant.

So the reason to progress the load depends on the goal. For muscle size, a hard set of 20 reps and
a hard set of 8 appear to do similar work, which is why adding reps can carry you a long way. For
strength, the load has to climb eventually — reps can build toward it, but a lifter who only ever
adds reps drifts out of the heavier range where strength is built.

That is also why a rep range has a top. Reps rise until they reach it, then the weight goes up and
the reps reset toward the bottom. This is often called double progression, and it captures both
findings: reps carry the growth, and the range keeps the load heavy enough for strength.

<aside class="summary">
<span class="summary-label">Summary</span>
<div class="summary-body"><p>With hard sets, muscle growth looks similar across light, moderate and heavy loads, but strength favours heavier loads. If strength is a goal, the load must rise eventually — which is what the top of a rep range is for.</p></div>
</aside>

<h2 id="how-hard">How hard each set needs to be</h2>

Progress depends on effort, but not on maximal effort every set. ACSM's 2026 position stand, an
overview of 137 systematic reviews that updates the 2009 stand, concludes that completing sets to
momentary muscular failure does not enhance gains in strength, size or power, so it is not
necessary for benefits to occur. [4] Its central recommendation is that healthy adults perform
progressive resistance training.

The research on proximity to failure fits that. A 2023 meta-analysis found only a trivial advantage
for training to failure over stopping short when it came to muscle growth, and concluded there was
no evidence that training to momentary failure is superior. [5] A 2024 analysis that estimated how
many reps were left in reserve found muscle growth improved as sets ended closer to failure, while
strength gains were similar across a wide range — but its authors call the work exploratory,
because reps in reserve had to be estimated from study descriptions. [6]

That points to a practical problem: nobody measures reps in reserve directly. A meta-analysis of
prediction accuracy found people tended to underestimate how many reps they had left, by about one
on average, with accuracy improving slightly closer to failure and in sets of 12 reps or fewer. [7]
Researchers also lack a consistent way of measuring proximity to failure, and studies define
failure itself differently, which makes precise targets hard to justify. [8]

The workable reading is this: most sets should be hard — within a few reps of failure — without
needing to reach it. And because "two reps in reserve" is a judgement rather than a measurement,
it is better treated as supporting evidence than as the trigger for adding weight.

<aside class="summary">
<span class="summary-label">Summary</span>
<div class="summary-body"><p>Training to failure is not required; most sets can stop a rep or two short. Reps in reserve is a useful but imperfect self-estimate — people tend to think they have fewer reps left than they do — so it works best as supporting evidence.</p></div>
</aside>

<h2 id="a-working-rule">A working rule</h2>

ACSM's 2009 stand gives the most-quoted progression rule: when a lifter can perform the current
load for one or two reps beyond the target in two consecutive sessions, increase the load by 2–10%,
using the lower end for small-muscle exercises and the higher end for large ones. [1] It is a
sensible rule, but worth seeing for what it is. ACSM graded it B — limited evidence — and it rests
on a single 1999 paper. [1] The percentages are practical guidance, not measured thresholds.

Our [Progressive Overload Calculator](/tools/progressive-overload/) turns that guidance into
explicit rules, and labels them as implementation rules rather than science:

- **The load goes up when every set reaches the top of your range** with technique intact and no pain. Until then, the answer is more reps at the same weight.
- **The increase is the smallest step your equipment allows** that lands within a band for the type of exercise — 2.5–5% for large and moderate compound lifts, 2–5% for isolation exercises, and 1–2.5% for small ones like lateral raises, following ACSM's advice to go lower on small-muscle exercises.
- **When even the smallest step is too large** — 15 to 20 lb dumbbells is a 33% jump — it keeps you adding reps, and allows the jump only once every set goes two reps past the top of the range.
- **Pain or a breakdown in technique vetoes any increase**, and severe pain stops the calculation entirely. No number of reps earns a heavier load through either.

The tool also asks for last session's reps, optionally, because a rule like ACSM's is about
consistency across sessions, not one good day — and it says so when it has less to go on.

<span class="summary-label">Summary</span>
<div class="summary-body"><p>ACSM suggests 2–10% load increases, lower for small muscles, once you beat your target in two sessions running — useful guidance with limited evidence behind it. A practical version: reps up to the top of the range, then the smallest sensible weight step, with pain and technique able to veto.</p></div>
</aside>

<h2 id="bottom-line">The bottom line</h2>

Progress is not the same as adding weight. Adding reps at the same load is progress, and for
muscle growth it appears to work about as well. Load matters more for strength, which is why reps
should eventually hand over to a heavier weight rather than climb forever.

Most of the time, train hard but short of failure, and treat your own sense of reps in reserve as
a rough guide rather than a precise number. Add weight when every set has reached the top of your
range, in the smallest step that makes sense for the exercise — and never let pain or failing
technique be the price of a heavier bar.
