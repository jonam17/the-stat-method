A food scale is the most accurate tool available to someone tracking intake, and
recommending one is easy. The difficulty is that a meaningful proportion of people stop
using it within a few weeks, and a tracking method with an adherence problem is not
actually accurate.

<h2 id="the-problem">Where weighing breaks down</h2>

Weighing works beautifully at home with food you prepared yourself. It works poorly at a
restaurant, at someone else's house, at a work lunch, while travelling, or on the kind of
week where cooking happens in a hurry.

Those situations are not edge cases. For most people they are a substantial fraction of all
meals, which means the accurate method covers only part of the diet and something has to
fill the gap.

The usual gap-filler is untrained visual estimation, and the research on that is not
flattering. Studies comparing self-reported intake against doubly labelled water have found
substantial underreporting.

The figure most often quoted — that people under-report by around 47 per cent — deserves its
context, because it is almost always given without any. It comes from a 1992 study of **ten
people**: nine women and one man, selected specifically because they reported eating under
1200 calories a day and were not losing weight. [2] That is a group chosen for the very
phenomenon being measured, which is the right design for the question the authors asked and
the wrong basis for a claim about people in general.

What the wider evidence supports is milder and still useful: under-reporting is common, it
grows with body size, and it is larger than most people expect. It is not a 47 per cent tax
on everyone's food diary. People are not lying either way; they are simply poor at judging
portions without a reference.

Which is the interesting part, because a reference is cheap. The idea behind hand portions —
use a familiar object you always have with you — has been tested directly. Researchers gave
young adults everyday objects as portion aids and had them estimate 36 foods with and without
them. [3] The finding that matters for us is not that the aids were perfect; it is that
purpose-built measurement tools work but are too bulky and expensive to actually carry, which
is precisely why an object you cannot forget is worth something even if it is cruder.

That is the honest case for hand portions: not accuracy, but the accuracy you will still be
getting in month six.

There is one more encouraging finding here. A controlled study compared dietitians against
non-dietitians, both keeping seven-day food records while their real expenditure was measured
by doubly labelled water. The non-dietitians under-reported significantly. The dietitians did
not — their records were close enough to their measured expenditure to be statistically
indistinguishable. [1]

The difference is not that dietitians have better eyes. It is practice and attention. Portion
estimation is a skill, and the gap between someone who has never calibrated and someone who
has is large enough to show up against a gold-standard measurement. Weighing your food for a
fortnight, then estimating, is not a compromise — it is how you become the second group.

<h2 id="how-it-works">How hand portions work</h2>

The system maps each macronutrient onto a part of your hand:

- **A palm** of protein — meat, fish, tofu, Greek yoghurt — delivers roughly 25 grams of protein.
- **A cupped handful** of carbohydrate — rice, pasta, oats, potato, fruit — roughly 25 grams of carbs.
- **A thumb** of fat — oil, nuts, nut butter, cheese — roughly 10 grams of fat.
- **A fist** of non-starchy vegetables, aiming for about one per meal.

The reason this works better than cups or spoons is that the reference scales with the
person. A larger individual has larger hands and generally needs more food; the unit adjusts
automatically. A cup is the same volume regardless of who is holding it.

The measure also travels. It is available at a restaurant, at a buffet, in an airport, and
in any situation where producing a scale would be socially peculiar.

<h2 id="accuracy">The accuracy trade, stated honestly</h2>

Hand portions are less accurate than a scale. A palm of chicken breast and a palm of salmon
differ in both protein and fat content. Hands vary in thickness as well as area. Cupped
handfuls of rice and of oats do not hold the same weight.

A reasonable expectation is ten to twenty percent error against weighed food. That sounds
significant until you compare it against the alternative it is actually competing with,
which is not a scale — it is untrained guessing, where errors are considerably larger.

Research on portion-size aids has found that giving people concrete visual references
improves estimation accuracy compared with unaided judgement. The hand is simply a reference
that is always present.

<div class="pull">
The right comparison is not "hands versus a scale". It is "hands versus whatever you would
actually do at a restaurant".
</div>

<h2 id="in-practice">Using the system well</h2>

**Calibrate once with a scale.** Weigh out a palm of chicken, a cupped handful of rice, and a
thumb of peanut butter. Look at them. That five-minute exercise anchors your reference far
better than reading a description.

**Recalibrate if you stall.** Portion creep is real — hands do not change, but what you count
as "a palm" drifts upward over months. If progress stops with everything else unchanged,
weigh food for a week to check.

**Use hands for structure and a scale for calorie-dense foods.** Oils, nut butters and cheese
carry the most calories per unit of volume and are the easiest to misjudge. Many people run
a hybrid: hands for protein and carbohydrate, a scale for fats at home.

**Do not mix systems within a meal without noticing.** Tracking half a meal precisely and
eyeballing the rest produces a number that feels accurate and is not.

<aside class="summary">
<span class="summary-label">Summary</span>
<div class="summary-body"><p>Calibrate once against a scale, recalibrate if progress stalls, and consider weighing calorie-dense fats even while using hands for everything else.</p></div>
</aside>

<h2 id="bottom-line">The bottom line</h2>

If you will genuinely weigh your food every day for months, do that — it is more accurate
and the tool exists for a reason.

If you know from experience that you will not, hand portions are not a compromise so much as
a realistic upgrade on what you would otherwise do. Calculate your macros, convert them to
palms, handfuls and thumbs, and let the method survive the parts of your life where a scale
never would.
