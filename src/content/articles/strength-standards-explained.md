"Am I strong?" is a question with no objective answer, which has not stopped the internet
from producing dozens of tables that confidently claim to provide one.

Strength standards are genuinely useful, but for a narrower purpose than most people use
them for.

<h2 id="what-they-are">What a standard actually is</h2>

Almost every strength standard expresses a lift as a multiple of bodyweight. A 2× bodyweight
squat means the same thing at 60 kilograms and at 100, even though the plates on the bar
differ substantially.

This is the same reasoning behind powerlifting coefficients: absolute load favours larger
lifters, so comparison needs normalising. Multiples of bodyweight are a cruder correction
than a fitted coefficient, but they are far easier to reason about and adequate for
orientation.

It is worth knowing where these thresholds come from, because it is not what most people
assume. The most-cited academic framework proposes classification bands built on exactly this
logic — a squat above roughly 1.5× bodyweight marking the top tier for men, for instance. [1]
But that is a **proposed model published in a practitioner journal**, offered as a common
vocabulary so researchers can describe their samples consistently. It is not a survey of what
people actually lift.

That distinction matters for how much weight to put on a label. A percentile tells you where
you sit in a measured population. A classification band tells you which side of a line
somebody drew for a different purpose. Both are useful; only one is a fact about the world.

Better tables also adjust for age and sex. Strength peaks somewhere in the late twenties and
declines gradually after, so comparing a fifty-year-old against an open-age standard is
comparing across a real physiological gap.

<div class="summary-body"><p>Standards normalise strength as a multiple of bodyweight so lifters of different sizes can be compared, with better tables also adjusting for age and sex.</p></div>

<h2 id="disagreement">Why every table gives a different answer</h2>

Look up "intermediate squat standard" across five sites and you will find five different
numbers, sometimes differing by half a bodyweight multiple.

The reason is that they are drawn from different populations. A table built on data from
people who log lifts in a training app reflects a self-selected group who are more committed
than average. A table built on general population testing reflects something else entirely.
A table assembled from a coach's clientele reflects that coach's clientele.

None of these is wrong. They are describing different groups, and the labels attached to
the bands — novice, intermediate, advanced — are editorial choices, not measurements.

<div class="pull">
Being "intermediate" on one site and "novice" on another does not mean one of them is
lying. It means the word has no fixed definition.
</div>

<h2 id="deceleration">Progress between levels is wildly uneven</h2>

The bands are not equally spaced in time, and this is the thing most people misread.

Getting from untrained to novice can take a few months of consistent training, and often
happens faster than expected because early strength gains are substantially neural — you are
learning to express force you already had. Novice to intermediate typically takes a year or
more. Intermediate to advanced can take several years, and many committed trainees never
reach the top band at all.

Meta-analytic work on training dose response is clear that the volume required to keep
progressing rises with training age, and that the rate of return falls. So a slowing rate of
progress is not evidence that your programme has stopped working. It is the expected shape
of a training career.

<span class="summary-label">Summary</span>
<div class="summary-body"><p>Early levels arrive quickly, partly through neural adaptation. Later ones take years, and many trainees never reach the top band. Slowing progress is normal, not a programme failure.</p></div>
</aside>

<h2 id="using-them">Using standards well</h2>

**As a next milestone.** The most useful thing a standard gives you is a concrete target
slightly above where you are. Our calculator shows the gap to the next level in kilograms,
which is a more actionable number than a label.

**As a balance check.** Comparing your bands across lifts is more informative than any single
lift's placement. An advanced deadlift alongside a novice overhead press is a signal about
your training history, and a reasonable prompt to redistribute effort.

**Not as an identity.** The band is a description of one number on one day. It says nothing
about your potential, your health, your work ethic, or whether your training is well
designed.

<aside class="summary">
<span class="summary-label">Summary</span>
<div class="summary-body"><p>Use standards to pick a next milestone and to check whether your lifts are balanced. Do not use them as a verdict on you as a trainee.</p></div>
</aside>

<h2 id="bottom-line">The bottom line</h2>

Strength standards are a map with an unreliable legend. They will tell you roughly where you
are and what the next landmark looks like, which is genuinely valuable.

What they will not tell you is whether you are training well, whether you are progressing
appropriately for your circumstances, or what you are capable of. Those questions are
answered by your own training log over months — not by a table.
