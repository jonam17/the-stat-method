How much protein to eat is covered in [our protein article](/articles/protein-how-much/). This one
is about what is inside it. Protein is made of amino acids, and proteins differ in which ones they
contain and how much of each the body can absorb. Those differences are what "protein quality"
means, and they are behind most of what is sold as leucine, BCAAs and "complete" protein.

<h2 id="what-amino-acids-are">Which amino acids you need</h2>

Muscle protein is built from twenty amino acids. The body can make eleven of them. The other nine
it cannot make in useful amounts, so they must come from food. [2] These are the essential, or
indispensable, amino acids: histidine, isoleucine, leucine, lysine, methionine, phenylalanine,
threonine, tryptophan and valine. [1]

Between the two groups sits a third. Cysteine, tyrosine, taurine, glycine, arginine, glutamine
and proline can become essential in particular physiological or medical conditions, which is why
the joint WHO, FAO and UNU expert report calls them conditionally indispensable. [1]

The reason the essential nine matter so much is simple. To build new muscle protein, every amino
acid it contains has to be available. A shortage of any essential one limits the whole process,
while the body can make up a shortage of the others itself. [2] So a protein is only as useful as
its scarcest essential amino acid, and that is the idea behind the standard quality score. [3]

How much of each do adults need? The expert report's best estimates, per kilogram of body weight
per day, include 39 mg of leucine and 30 mg of lysine, and 184 mg for all nine together. These sit
alongside an average protein requirement of 0.66 g per kilogram. [1] For a 70 kg adult, that works
out to about 2.7 g of leucine and 13 g of essential amino acids a day. The report is open about
the uncertainty. Most of these figures are about twice those in its 1985 predecessor, and they
have not been properly tested in long-term studies of people eating those amounts. [1]

Those amounts are small compared with what most diets supply. In the report's comparison, egg,
beef, milk, soya, potato, rice, maize and wheat proteins all exceed the requirement for nearly
every essential amino acid. The exceptions are lysine in cereals, at 57% to 86% of the
requirement, lysine in cassava and yam, at just over 90%, and the branched-chain amino acids in
cassava. [1]

<span class="summary-label">Summary</span>
<div class="summary-body"><p>Nine amino acids must come from food, and building muscle protein needs all of them at once. Adult requirements for each are small, and most protein foods exceed them; cereals, which are low in lysine, are the main exception.</p></div>

<h2 id="scoring-protein-quality">How protein quality is scored</h2>

For years, the standard score has been the protein digestibility-corrected amino acid score, or
PDCAAS. It takes the essential amino acid in shortest supply in a protein, compares it with a
reference pattern of requirements, and multiplies the result by how much of the protein is
digested. Any value above 100% is cut back to 100%. [3]

Without that cap, the scores spread out. In one published table, egg scores 118%, cow's milk 121%,
beef 92%, soy 91% and wheat 42%. [3] With the cap, egg, milk and soy protein isolate all score
1.00, while beef scores 0.92, soy 0.91, pea 0.67 and whole wheat 0.45. [4]

Writing in 2000, one critic set out three problems with PDCAAS. The reference pattern then in use
came from studies of a small number of two-year-olds recovering from malnutrition. Digestion was
measured from what is left in the stool, but amino acids that reach the large intestine are most
likely lost to the body, so what has been absorbed by the end of the small intestine is the better
measure. And the cap makes milk and soy look the same, even though milk carries more of the amino
acids that can make up for gaps in other foods; capping makes sense only when one protein is the
whole diet. [3] That critique came from a scientist at a dairy company, at a symposium sponsored
by dairy organisations. [3] The 2007 expert report set new reference patterns for each age group,
agreed that amino acid digestibility is better measured at the end of the small intestine, called
the cap controversial, and left a full table of updated scores to a later report. [1]

In 2011, an FAO expert consultation recommended a replacement: the digestible indispensable amino
acid score, or DIAAS. It checks every essential amino acid separately, each corrected for how much
of it is absorbed by the end of the small intestine, and the lowest sets the score. Scores above
100% are kept for single foods, so a protein that can make up for gaps in others gets credit for
it. They are capped only when scoring a whole diet, or a food that is someone's only protein, such
as infant formula. [9] In a worked example in the report, using four amino acids, whole milk
powder scores 143% against the pattern for older children and adults. For food labels, it gave
example cut-offs: 100% or more for "excellent" or "high", 75% to 99% for "good" or "source", and
no protein claim below 75%, while saying the actual values needed more thought. [9] There is a catch. The
consultation found too little data on digestibility at the end of the small intestine to use it in
practice yet. Until there is, it said, DIAAS should be worked out from protein digestibility
measured in the stool, as PDCAAS is. [9]

Mixing proteins changes the score, because one food's surplus can cover another's shortfall. The
expert report works through an example. A mixture of 400 g wheat, 100 g chickpea and 35 g milk
powder scores 0.84 for adults. That score is set by lysine, the amino acid wheat is short of,
together with the mixture's digestibility. [1]

There is a bigger limit. These scores estimate how well a protein meets the minimum needed to
prevent deficiency. They do not measure how well it builds muscle. [4] Soybeans and beef score
almost the same, at 0.91 and 0.92. Yet beef raised muscle protein synthesis more than the same
amount of protein from a soy-based beef substitute, and isolated soy protein has done less than
whey, milk or beef protein in several studies, at rest and after exercise. [4]

<aside class="summary">
<span class="summary-label">Summary</span>
<div class="summary-body"><p>PDCAAS rates a protein by its scarcest essential amino acid and its digestibility, and caps every good protein at the same score. DIAAS, recommended to replace it, scores each amino acid separately and keeps scores above 100% for single foods. Both measure whether minimum needs are met, not how well a protein supports muscle.</p></div>
</aside>

<h2 id="leucine-and-bcaas">Leucine, the dose per meal, and BCAAs</h2>

Leucine gets special attention because it is not only a building block. It is also thought to act
as a signal that switches on muscle protein synthesis. [2] [4] The popular idea of a "leucine
threshold" follows from this: that a meal needs a certain amount of leucine to switch synthesis
on fully. What the trials show is less tidy.

The clearest test of the dose is a trial in 48 resistance-trained young men weighing about 80 kg.
Three hours after a breakfast with protein, they trained their legs and then drank 0, 10, 20 or
40 g of whey protein. Over the next four hours, averaged across the trained and the rested leg,
20 g raised muscle protein synthesis by about 49% above the drink with no protein, and 40 g by
about 56%. The two were not significantly different, and 10 g did no significantly better than
none. [5] The extra amino acids in the 40 g drink were mostly broken down and excreted. [5] The
drink was 11% leucine, so 20 g carried about 2.2 g of leucine and 10 g of essential amino acids. [5]

Read that result for what it is. The authors could not rule out a 14% increase with 40 g, and they
point out that the right dose may depend on muscle mass, age, sex and training. [5]
GlaxoSmithKline, which made the whey drink, funded the trial and took part in its design, analysis
and interpretation. [5]

Older adults appear to need more per meal. The authors of a trial in women aged 65 to 75 cite
earlier work in which older men needed about two-thirds more protein per meal than younger men.
Scaling that to their participants, who weighed about 72 kg, they estimated about 29 g of protein,
or 3.5 g of leucine, assuming women and men need the same. [6]

That trial, in 22 women, tested leucine directly. The women drank 15 g of protein twice a day,
with either 4.2 g or 1.3 g of leucine per drink. After one drink, muscle protein synthesis rose
53% in the rested leg with the higher-leucine drink and 13% with the other. Over six days, it rose
in the rested leg only with the higher-leucine drink. [6] Two cautions apply. The comparison drink
also differed in its protein blend, which was part soy. And PepsiCo funded the study and employed
four of its authors, though the paper says they had no role in the data analysis. [6]

Leucine on its own is a different matter. Taking extra leucine lowers the blood levels of the other
two branched-chain amino acids, valine and isoleucine, which may then run short. [2] In one
long-term trial, the fall levelled off within the normal range. [4] In two trials from one
review author's laboratory, 7.5 g of leucine a day for three and six months did not measurably
increase muscle mass or strength in older men, either healthy or with type 2 diabetes. The authors
note that these men were already active and eating more than 1 g of protein per kilogram a day. [4]

The same reasoning applies to BCAA supplements, which supply leucine, isoleucine and valine without
the other six essential amino acids. A 2017 review found no study that had measured muscle protein
synthesis after BCAAs were taken by mouth on their own, and the two studies that infused them into
a vein found that synthesis fell. [2] Its author concludes that BCAAs alone do not build muscle. He
has received grants or fees from a beef checkoff programme and from Abbott, Danone and PepsiCo,
and owns shares in a company called Essential Blends. [2]

A trial published the same year did measure it. Ten resistance-trained young men each came in
twice, about three weeks apart. Each time, after breakfast, they trained one leg and then drank
either 5.6 g of BCAAs, the amount in about 20 g of whey, or a placebo. Over the next four hours, muscle protein synthesis was 22% higher after the
BCAAs. [10] The authors note that this is about half the rise earlier studies found with whey
carrying a similar amount of BCAAs. They conclude that BCAAs alone do not produce the full
response, most likely because the other essential amino acids run short. [10] The trial was small,
and GlaxoSmithKline, which supplied the drinks, funded it. [10]

<aside class="summary">
<span class="summary-label">Summary</span>
<div class="summary-body"><p>About 20 g of whey, carrying about 2.2 g of leucine, was enough to make muscle building plateau in trained young men; older adults appear to need more. Leucine or BCAAs taken without the other essential amino acids did less than whole protein, and have not been shown to add muscle over time.</p></div>
</aside>

<h2 id="plant-animal-collagen">Plant, animal and collagen proteins</h2>

A Dutch laboratory measured the amino acids in 35 commercial protein powders. On average, the
essential amino acids made up 26% of plant proteins, 37% of animal proteins and 38% of human
muscle. [7] Their method could not measure tryptophan, so these totals leave it out. Leucine
averaged 7.1% in the plant proteins and 8.8% in the animal ones. Plant proteins were also lower in
lysine (3.6% against 7.0%) and methionine (1.0% against 2.5%). [7]

The averages hide a wide spread. Leucine ran from 5.1% in hemp protein to 13.5% in corn protein,
higher than the 11.0% the same lab measured in whey. Potato protein was the only plant protein
that met the requirement for every essential amino acid. [7] Here is how much of each protein it
would take to get the 2.7 g of leucine in 25 g of whey: [7]

| Protein | Protein needed for 2.7 g leucine |
|---|---|
| Whey | 25 g |
| Corn | 20 g |
| Potato | 33 g |
| Brown rice | 37 g |
| Pea | 38 g |
| Soy | 40 g |
| Wheat | 45 g |
| Oat | 47 g |
| Hemp | 54 g |

Combining plant proteins helps. Corn, hemp and brown rice are low in lysine, while soy and pea are
low in methionine. Getting every essential amino acid from one of these alone would take two to
four times the usual protein requirement. A 50/50 blend of one from each group would take only
10% to 90% more. [7] The samples were isolates supplied to the lab by their makers. Two of the
authors have received speaking or consulting fees from FrieslandCampina and Nutricia, and the
project's industry partners took part in regular discussions. [7]

Whether these differences matter for muscle over months is less certain. In some training trials,
plant protein produced smaller lean-mass gains than dairy. In others, where people ate larger
amounts of plant protein, such as 48 g of rice protein after each session, the gains were
similar. [4] The review that brings these together argues that plant protein can support muscle
growth if people eat more of it, above 30 g a meal. It also notes how few plant proteins have
been tested at all, mainly soy and wheat. [4]

Collagen sits at the bottom of the scale. It contains no tryptophan, so its digestible
indispensable amino acid score is zero. [8] In a trial in 22 women aged around 69, 30 g of whey or
collagen was taken twice a day for six days. Each whey serving carried 4.3 g of leucine and 15.4 g
of essential amino acids. Each collagen serving carried 0.9 g and 5.6 g. Over the six days, muscle
protein synthesis rose with whey, at rest and after exercise, but not significantly with collagen
in either. [8] The trial was small. Samples from seven women were lost to a laboratory equipment
failure, so the muscle results rest on eight women given whey and seven given collagen. [8]
Whether collagen helps joints, skin or tendons is a separate question, for a separate article.

<aside class="summary">
<span class="summary-label">Summary</span>
<div class="summary-body"><p>Plant proteins contain less of the essential amino acids on average, but some match or beat animal proteins on particular ones, and blends or larger servings close much of the gap. Collagen lacks tryptophan and did not raise muscle building in older women.</p></div>
</aside>

<h2 id="bottom-line">The bottom line</h2>

Nine amino acids have to come from food, and muscle needs all of them together. Most protein foods
supply more than the minimum, and mixing foods covers the gaps of any single one. [1]

Quality scores tell you whether a protein meets minimum needs. They do not tell you how well it
builds muscle. [4] For that, what has been measured points to the amount of leucine and the other
essential amino acids in a meal. In trained young men, about 20 g of whey was enough, and more was
mostly broken down. [5] Older adults appear to need more. [6]

None of this supports buying leucine or BCAAs on their own. The evidence points to the essential
amino acids working as a set, which whole protein supplies. [2] [4]

If you eat mostly plant protein, eat a little more of it and mix sources. [4] [7]

and it is best met from more than one source.
