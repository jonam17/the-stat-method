A routine is the part of training people argue about most, and the part where the evidence is
simplest. Full-body three times a week, an upper/lower split, push/pull/legs, a day per body part: each
has loyal followers, and each is usually defended by pointing at someone who got big or strong doing
it.

The useful question is narrower. Our guides to [training volume](/articles/training-volume-how-many-sets-do-you-actually-need/)
and [training frequency](/articles/training-frequency-and-rest-days/) cover what a week needs to
contain. A routine is how you arrange that week. This article covers what any arrangement has to
achieve, how the common ones distribute the work, what trials comparing them directly have found,
and how to choose one that fits your time.

<h2 id="what-a-routine-does">What a routine has to do</h2>

Start with the floor. The World Health Organization's 2020 guidelines recommend that adults do
muscle-strengthening activities at moderate or greater intensity, involving all major muscle groups,
on two or more days a week. [1] It is a strong recommendation, supported by moderate-certainty
evidence. [1] For adults over 65, the guidelines recommend varied multicomponent activity that
emphasises balance and strength training on three or more days a week. [1]

That is a health minimum, not a plan for getting as strong or as muscular as you can. For that, two
quantities matter more than the layout: how many hard sets each muscle gets per week, and, for
strength, how often you practise the lifts you care about. Both are covered in the guides linked
above; the short version is that more weekly sets bring more growth with diminishing returns, and
that strength benefits from training a lift more than once a week.

A narrative review of time-efficient training puts a practical lower bound on the first quantity:
at least four weekly sets per muscle group, adding that ten or more may bring further muscle growth
for those with the time. [2] The same review notes that when weekly volume is matched,
how often a muscle is trained makes little difference to strength or size, and that frequency
matters in practice mainly because more sessions make room for more sets. [2]

So a routine has three jobs: cover every major muscle group, deliver the weekly sets you are aiming
for, and do it in sessions you will keep doing.

<h2 id="the-layouts">The four common layouts</h2>

Every routine is a way of dividing muscle groups across sessions. The four most common:

- **Full-body.** Every session trains every major muscle group, usually with one to three exercises
  each. Typically two to four days a week.
- **Upper/lower.** Sessions alternate between upper-body and lower-body days. Typically four days a
  week.
- **Push/pull/legs (PPL).** One session for pressing muscles (chest, shoulders, triceps), one for pulling
  muscles (back, biceps), one for legs. Run once or twice through in a week: three or six days.
- **Body-part split.** One or two muscle groups per session — chest day, back day, leg day, and so
  on. Typically five days a week.

The difference that matters is how often each muscle comes round, and therefore how many sets each
session has to carry. Take a target of 12 hard sets per muscle per week. Per week, by layout:

| Layout | Days | Sessions per muscle | Sets per session |
|---|---|---|---|
| Full-body | 3 | 3 | 4 |
| Upper/lower | 4 | 2 | 6 |
| PPL, twice | 6 | 2 | 6 |
| PPL, once | 3 | 1 | 12 |
| Body-part | 5 | 1 | 12 |

The weekly total is the same in every row. What changes is the shape of each session: four sets per
muscle across every muscle group, or twelve sets for one or two muscle groups. A full-body session
touches everything lightly; a body-part session works one area hard. Neither is a better stimulus in
itself, but the shape decides how long sessions run, how tired a muscle is by its last sets, and
what happens to your week when you miss a day.

<h2 id="head-to-head">What the head-to-head trials found</h2>

Several trials have compared split and full-body routines directly. The informative ones match the
weekly sets, so that only the arrangement differs.

A 2024 meta-analysis pooled 14 studies with 392 participants, all healthy adults, comparing split
and full-body routines. [3] It found no significant difference in strength gains, either in the
bench press (mean difference 1.19, interval −1.28 to 3.65) or in lower-body strength (2.47, −2.11
to 7.05). [3] Measures of muscle size — the cross-sectional area of the elbow flexors and
extensors, the vastus lateralis of the thigh, and lean body mass — showed no difference either. [3]
The authors concluded that when volume is equated, the choice between the two does not affect
strength or muscle growth, and that people can pick on personal preference. [3]

The individual trials fill in what that looks like:

- **Untrained men.** In a Brazilian trial, 67 young men who had not lifted in the previous six months
  trained four times a week for eight weeks. [4] One group did an A/B split, training each muscle twice a week; the other did
  full-body sessions, training each muscle four times a week. Both did 16 sets per muscle group per
  week, at 8 to 12 repetitions taken close to failure. [4] Both groups gained about 18% on the bench
  press and 28% on the squat, and muscle thickness rose in all four muscles measured, with no
  difference between groups. [4] Two of the authors declared that they sell clinics and workshops on the topic. [4]
- **Untrained women.** In a trial of 50 young women who had not strength-trained in the previous six
  months, participants were randomised to two full-body sessions or four upper/lower sessions a week
  for 12 weeks. [5] Each muscle was trained
  twice a week either way, with the same exercises, sets and repetitions. [5] Strength in the bench
  press, lat pulldown and leg press, jump height, power and muscle mass all improved, with no
  significant differences between the groups. [5] One difference was practical: of the six women who
  dropped out, five were in the four-day group, and the authors give lack of time as the main
  reason. [5]
- **Trained men.** In a ten-week trial of 21 resistance-trained men, both a total-body and a
  split routine improved every strength and muscle-thickness measure. [6] The total-body group
  improved more in one of two isokinetic bench press tests, and the split group gained more thickness
  in the vastus lateralis. [6] The published abstract does not say whether weekly sets were matched. The authors suggested total-body training may
  suit maximal strength and split training muscle growth, but with 21 men and two differences among
  many measures, that is a hypothesis, not a finding to build a programme on.

The limits of this evidence are worth stating. The three trials described here lasted eight to
twelve weeks, two of them were in people new to lifting, and comparisons with matched weekly sets are
by design the ones least likely to find a difference. What they show is that the arrangement is not
a hidden lever: given the same work, the body does not much care how the work is grouped.

The trials were short; one in trained men found small, mixed differences.</p></div>

<h2 id="fitting-it-in">Fitting it into your week</h2>

If the layout does not change results directly, it should be chosen for how well it fits your life.
A few practical points follow from the evidence and the arithmetic above.

**Count your realistic training days first.** Two or three days a week points to full-body
sessions: of the four layouts, it is the only one that trains every muscle at least twice in that time, which also
gives the lifts you care about more practice. Four days fits upper/lower or full-body. Five or six
days opens up push/pull/legs or a body-part split. The days you can reliably keep matter more than
the days you can manage in a good week, and the dropouts in the trial in women suggest more sessions
can be harder to keep up. [5]

**Think about missed sessions.** A missed full-body session costs every muscle a third or a quarter
of its week. A missed leg day on a body-part split costs the legs the whole week. If your schedule is
unpredictable, layouts that train each muscle more often absorb a missed day better.

**Mind the session length.** High weekly volumes in few sessions make for long sessions: 20 sets for
a muscle group on a once-a-week split is 20 sets in one go. If sessions are running long, adding a
day is usually better than cramming.

**When time is short, keep the efficient core.** The time-efficiency review recommends prioritising
bilateral, multi-joint exercises through a full range of motion, with at least one leg-pressing
exercise such as a squat, one upper-body pull such as a pull-up, and one upper-body push such as a
bench press. [2] It recommends loads of about 6 to 15 repetition maximum, rest of 1 to 2 minutes
between sets for beginners and at least 2 minutes for trained lifters, and warming up with lighter
sets of the exercise itself rather than a long general warm-up. [2] Supersets, drop sets and
rest-pause sets roughly halve training time while keeping the volume, but the evidence for them is
limited, they appear better suited to muscle growth than maximal strength, and the authors advise
against drop sets on heavy compound free-weight lifts such as squats. [2] Most of the research the
review draws on was in untrained or recreationally active people. [2]

**Keep something going when life gets busy.** In one study the review describes, people who cut back
to one or two sessions a week after ten weeks of leg-extension training kept their strength; in
another, once-weekly training maintained one-rep max strength for 32 weeks in both younger and older
men, though the older men lost the muscle they had gained while the younger men kept it. [2] A busy
stretch is better spent on a short maintenance routine than on stopping altogether.

<h2 id="bottom-line">The bottom line</h2>

There is no best split. The trials that have compared them directly, with weekly work matched, found
similar gains from full-body and split routines.

**Decide the week, then the layout.** Set the weekly sets you want per muscle, then choose the
layout that fits them into the days you have without sessions that drag on.

**Two or three days a week favours full-body.** Of these layouts, it is the only one that trains
every muscle more than once a week in that time, and it absorbs a missed day best.

**More days opens more options.** Upper/lower, push/pull/legs and body-part splits all work; pick
the one you enjoy enough to repeat for months, and train the lifts you most want to improve at least
twice a week.
