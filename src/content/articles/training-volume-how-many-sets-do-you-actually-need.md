Ask how many sets you should do and you will usually get a number: ten a week, or twelve to
twenty, or "as many as you can recover from". Each of those has a study behind it. None of them
is wrong, exactly. But each is a point taken from a curve, and the shape of the curve is more
useful than any single point on it.

The short version is that volume works, that it works with sharply diminishing returns, and that
it works very differently for muscle size than for strength. The longer version explains why a
lifter doing a third of the sets of the person next to them can be gaining strength just as fast.

<h2 id="what-counts">What counts as a set</h2>

"Volume" means two different things, and mixing them up causes most of the confusion.

The older meaning is **volume load**: sets × reps × weight. Five sets of ten at 60 kg is 3,000 kg.
It is useful for tracking a single lift over time, but it is a poor way to compare programmes,
because it rewards light weights and high reps. Doubling the reps at half the weight leaves volume
load unchanged and produces a completely different session.

The research this article draws on counts something simpler: **sets per muscle per week**. That is
the dose in every study below. It only works as a measure because the sets being counted are hard.
In the trials that built the evidence, most sets were taken to or near failure — in the largest
pooled analysis, about 78 per cent of the effects for muscle size came from protocols with some
definition of failure, though only about 30 per cent defined it clearly. [2] A set that ends ten reps short of failure is not the same dose as one
that ends two short, and counting them the same would make the whole measure meaningless.

That matters most for muscle size. An analysis pooling studies that varied proximity to failure
found that growth increased as sets were stopped closer to failure, while strength gains were
similar across a wide range of reps in reserve. [5] The authors are careful to call the analysis
exploratory, because how close each set came to failure had to be estimated from study
descriptions rather than measured. If you train by RPE, the practical translation is in our
[guide to reps in reserve](/articles/rpe-training-guide/): most working sets at two or three reps
short of failure count as hard sets.

There is a second counting problem. A bench press trains the chest directly, but the triceps and
front shoulders work too. Does a set of bench press count as a set for the triceps? The 2025
meta-regression tested three answers — count indirect sets as a full set, as half a set, or not
at all — and found the data fitted best when a set for a muscle that assists, rather than drives,
the movement counted as **half a set**. [2] The authors call this a heuristic for modelling, not a
rule, and say the right weighting probably varies by exercise. But it is a sensible default: four
sets of rows are roughly two sets for your biceps.

<span class="summary-label">Summary</span>
<div class="summary-body"><p>The research measures volume as hard sets per muscle per week, not total weight lifted. Sets need to be reasonably close to failure to count, especially for muscle growth, and sets where a muscle only assists are best counted as about half a set.</p></div>
</aside>

<h2 id="the-curve">The dose-response curve</h2>

The modern case for volume starts with a 2017 meta-analysis by Brad Schoenfeld and colleagues.
Pooling 15 studies and 34 training groups, it found that each additional weekly set was
associated with about 0.37 percentage points more muscle growth. [1] Grouped into bands, fewer
than five weekly sets per muscle produced an average gain of 5.4 per cent, five to nine sets
6.6 per cent, and ten or more 9.8 per cent.

That last comparison is the source of the widely repeated "ten sets a week" figure, and it is
weaker than its fame suggests. The difference between the three bands was a trend, not a
statistically significant result (P = 0.074). When the authors removed one influential study — a
six-month trial in which the highest-volume group did 30 sets a week — the advantage of ten-plus
sets over five to nine **disappeared**, and the per-set effect fell to 0.25 percentage points. [1]
Only two of the fifteen studies used people with lifting experience, and so few groups did more
than twelve weekly sets that the authors said they could not tell where the benefit levelled
off. Their own reading was balanced: low-volume training of four or fewer weekly sets still
produced substantial growth, and more volume produced more.

Eight years and many more trials later, a 2025 meta-regression from Florida Atlantic University
pooled 67 studies and 2,058 participants and tested what *shape* the relationship takes, rather
than just whether more is better. [2] For muscle size, the best-fitting curve showed growth rising
with volume but flattening as it went. The authors expressed this as the number of extra sets
needed for each additional increment of growth large enough to be detected over measurement
error:

| Weekly sets (half-counting indirect) | Extra sets needed for each further detectable gain |
|---|---|
| About 4 | The minimum that produced detectable growth |
| 5–10 | About 6 |
| 11–18 | About 8.5 |
| 19–29 | About 10.75 |
| 30–42 | About 12.5 |
| 43 and above | Too few studies to say |

Read the table as a price list. The first few sets buy a lot. By the high twenties you are paying
nearly twice as many sets for the same increment. The curve never actually stopped rising in
their data, but the authors stress that few studies tested more than about 25 sets, so the upper
end is uncertain — it is compatible with a plateau, or even with too much volume becoming
counterproductive. [2]

<aside class="summary">
<span class="summary-label">Summary</span>
<div class="summary-body"><p>More weekly sets produce more muscle growth, but each additional increment costs more sets than the last. Detectable growth starts at around four hard sets a week, and the evidence above about 25 sets is thin.</p></div>
</aside>

<h2 id="strength">Strength is a different curve</h2>

The most useful single study on this question is Schoenfeld's 2019 trial in trained men, because
it tested very different volumes on people who already lifted. [3] Forty-five men averaging four
years of lifting experience started, and thirty-four completed eight weeks of a seven-exercise full-body programme,
three times a week, with every set taken to failure in the 8–12 rep range. The only difference
between groups was sets per exercise: one, three or five. For the legs, that came to 9, 27 and 45
weekly sets.

Muscle growth followed the dose. The five-set group gained significantly more thickness than the
one-set group at the front of the thigh, the outer thigh and the elbow flexors. [3]

Strength did not. Squat and bench press gains showed no significant difference between groups,
and the statistical evidence favoured *no* difference for the squat. [3]

<figure data-chart="volume-squat-strength"></figure>

The practical consequence is in the session lengths the paper reports: about 13 minutes for one
set per exercise, 40 for three and 68 for five. [3] For strength, the shortest programme was as
good as one more than five times as long. The authors did note limits: the study was slightly
underpowered after eleven dropouts, it lasted eight weeks, it used moderate rep ranges rather than
heavy strength-specific ones, and most of the men had not been training to failure before, so the
change itself may have been a stimulus.

The 2025 meta-regression points the same way, and more strongly. [2] For strength, a single
weekly set of the tested exercise — counting closely related exercises as half — was enough to
produce a detectable gain, and further detectable improvements stopped at around four sets.
Beyond that, extra volume did not consistently add gains larger than measurement error: in the
authors' words, a functional plateau. Two caveats apply. Their threshold for a detectable strength
gain was nearly 4 per cent, which is a lot to a powerlifter, so smaller benefits beyond four sets
may well exist. And the authors suspect many participants were tested on lifts they had not practised much,
so early gains partly reflect learning the movement.

The idea that ties this together is **progressive overload**: you keep improving by gradually
asking more of the body over time, through heavier loads, more reps or more sets. For strength,
the pooled data suggest that practising the lift more often pays off more reliably than piling on
sets: weekly frequency had a clear positive effect on strength, with diminishing returns, while
it made little measurable difference to muscle size. [2] For size, adding sets is one of the levers that keeps working longest.

<aside class="summary">
<span class="summary-label">Summary</span>
<div class="summary-body"><p>In trained men, one set per exercise matched five sets for strength over eight weeks, while more sets produced more muscle growth. Pooled data agree: strength gains level off after a few weekly sets, while muscle size keeps responding to more.</p></div>
</aside>

<h2 id="the-numbers">Where the popular numbers come from</h2>

Three numbers circulate, and each is worth knowing the source of.

**"At least ten sets a week."** This is the practical recommendation of the 2017 meta-analysis.
[1] As above, it rests on a trend that lost its advantage when one study was removed, from a pool
in which only two of fifteen studies used experienced lifters. It is a reasonable target, not a
threshold.

**"Twelve to twenty sets a week."** This comes from a 2022 review that compared moderate (12–20)
with high (more than 20) weekly volumes in young, trained men. [4] It included seven studies, six
of them in the pooled analysis. Higher volume produced significantly more growth in the triceps,
but not in the quadriceps (P = 0.19) or biceps (P = 0.59). The authors concluded that 12 to 20 sets
may be an optimal standard recommendation. Notice what that conclusion rests on: a failure to
find a difference in two muscles across a handful of small studies. That is absence of evidence,
not evidence of a ceiling — and the pooled estimates for both muscles still leaned towards the
higher volumes.

**"Forty-five sets."** The highest volume in the 2019 trial produced the most leg growth. [3] It
also took over an hour per session, three times a week, and was tested for eight weeks. That is
evidence that very high volume can work in the short term for young trained men, not that it is
sustainable or wise for anyone else.

Two things none of these studies tell you are worth stating plainly.

**Who was studied.** The 2025 analysis was 79 per cent male with an average age of 25, and it
excluded adults over 70. [2] The 2019 trial was entirely young men. [3] The 2017 analysis did
include women and older adults and found no evidence that they responded to volume differently
— but with only fifteen studies, the absence of a difference is weak evidence. [1]

**What high volume costs.** The 2025 authors say directly that they did not examine injury,
sustainability or burnout, and point out that every participant in their data had to tolerate
the programme well enough to finish it. [2] Studies averaged about ten weeks. Two of the eleven
dropouts in the 2019 trial left with training-related injuries; the paper's text does not break
them down by group. [3] The upside of more sets is measured carefully. The downside largely
is not.

<aside class="summary">
<span class="summary-label">Summary</span>
<div class="summary-body"><p>The 10-set and 12–20-set figures are reasonable targets drawn from limited evidence, mostly in young men over a few months. None of the research measured the costs of high volume, so its upper limits describe what worked briefly for people who could tolerate it.</p></div>
</aside>

<h2 id="bottom-line">The bottom line</h2>

The evidence supports a simple way of thinking about sets, rather than a number to hit.

**For strength,** a few hard weekly sets of the lift you want to improve get most of the benefit.
If progress stalls, practising the lift more often is a better-supported next step than adding
sets.

**For muscle size,** about four hard sets per muscle per week is enough to make progress, and
somewhere around ten is a sensible place for most people to sit. More can work, but each extra
set buys less than the one before, and above the mid-twenties the evidence gets thin.

**For everyone,** add volume gradually and judge it by results: whether your lifts and
measurements are moving, and whether you recover between sessions. If they stall and you are
recovering well, a few more sets is a reasonable next step. If you are not recovering, more sets
are not the answer. The best volume is the one you can repeat for months, not the one that won an
eight-week study.
