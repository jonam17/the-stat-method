Ask three sources how much protein to eat and you'll get three answers that differ by a
factor of three. The official floor sits near 0.8 g per kilogram of bodyweight. [3] It is
worth knowing what that number is and is not: it is the amount at which nitrogen balance
studies found most healthy adults stop losing protein — a threshold for avoiding deficiency,
not an amount optimal for anything. It was also rounded down from 0.83, and later work using
different methods has argued it is set too low, particularly for older adults. Treating it as
a target rather than a floor is the single most common misreading of it. Lifting
forums say two grams per pound. The supplement label suggests whatever finishes the tub
fastest.

All three are answering different questions. The first is the amount that prevents
deficiency in a sedentary adult. The second is a bulking-era heuristic that survived
because overshooting protein is fairly harmless in healthy adults. Neither is aimed at someone training
hard and trying to keep muscle in a deficit.

<h2 id="start-from-lean-mass-not-scale-weight">Bodyweight or lean mass?</h2>

Protein ranges come in two units, and mixing them up is an easy mistake. The everyday range
for people who exercise, and the meta-analysis behind the familiar 1.6, are both per kilogram
of bodyweight. [5] [1] The range for lean athletes in a calorie deficit is per kilogram of lean
mass, which is bodyweight minus fat. [2] A lean-mass figure applied to bodyweight overshoots,
and a bodyweight figure applied to lean mass falls short.

Our calculator follows the sources. It uses bodyweight outside a deficit, and lean mass in a
deficit when you give it your body fat.

<h2 id="the-range-that-actually-matters">The range that actually matters</h2>

For most people who exercise, the International Society of Sports Nutrition puts the daily
range at 1.4 to 2.0 g per kilogram of bodyweight. [5] In a calorie deficit, lean
resistance-trained people may need more: 2.3 to 3.1 g per kilogram of lean mass. [2]

Where the lower figure comes from is worth knowing precisely, because it is quoted
everywhere as though it were exact. The meta-analysis pooling 49 trials and 1,863 lifters
put the plateau at **1.62 g per kilogram of bodyweight a day** — but with a 95% confidence interval running from 1.03
all the way to 2.20, and the breakpoint itself falling short of conventional statistical
significance. [1] The widely repeated "1.6" is the centre of a wide interval, not a
threshold. That is precisely why a *band* is the honest way to express this and a single
number is not.

<figure data-chart="protein-breakpoint"></figure>

Two further caveats from the same paper. The benefit shrank with age and was larger in
already-trained lifters. And the senior author declares grant support and honoraria from the
US National Dairy Council, which funded trials included in the analysis — a conflict we
disclose here on the same footing as the ones noted in our creatine article, because the
standard should not move depending on which way it points.

- **Maintenance or a surplus:** 1.4 to 2.0 g per kilogram of bodyweight. [5]
- **In a deficit:** 2.3 to 3.1 g per kilogram of lean mass, higher the harder the cut and the
  leaner you are. [2]
- **Older trainees:** in the meta-analysis, extra protein helped less with age, not more. [1]

The deficit range deserves its own caveat. It comes from recommendations written for
**natural bodybuilding contest preparation**, competitors driving toward stage-level leanness
under sustained deficits, and the authors note they had to write a narrative rather than
systematic review because the evidence base was so thin. [2] By their own reasoning, the
lower end is the one that fits someone carrying more fat or cutting gently.

Kidney disease is a different question. As the body uses protein, it produces waste that the
kidneys remove, and some people with chronic kidney disease need a moderate amount of protein
so that waste doesn't build up in the blood. People on dialysis may need more, because dialysis
removes protein from the blood. Too little protein brings its own risk, malnutrition, so the
right amount is one to work out with a dietitian or doctor rather than from these ranges. [4]

<h2 id="distribution-matters-less-than-the-total">Distribution matters less than the total</h2>

The "30 g per meal absorption limit" is a persistent myth. Your body handles larger single
doses fine; the total across the day is what drives outcomes. Spreading intake across three
or four meals is convenient and probably marginally better for muscle protein synthesis,
but it isn't the lever people think it is.

Hit the daily number first. Optimise timing afterwards, if ever.
