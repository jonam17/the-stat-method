VO2 max has become something like a fitness score, shown on watches and in apps. It is a real
physiological measurement, first defined in the 1920s. [1] It is closely linked to health, and it is
much harder to estimate than a watch makes it look.

This article covers what VO2 max is and what limits it, what the health research does and does not
show, how far the common estimates can be trusted, and what kind of training raises it.

<h2 id="what-it-measures">What VO2 max measures</h2>

VO2 max is the highest rate at which your body can take up and use oxygen during severe exercise. [1]
It is one of the central measurements in exercise physiology, the usual indicator of
cardiorespiratory fitness, and the most common way studies show that training worked. [1] It can be
given in litres per minute, but it is usually divided by body weight and given in millilitres per
kilogram per minute (mL/kg/min). [2]

The range is wide. Measured values run from under 10 mL/kg/min in people with advanced chronic
disease to over 80 in young elite endurance athletes. [3] The type of test matters too: in untrained
people, a cycling test gives values 10 to 20 per cent lower than a treadmill test. [3]

**What sets the limit.** Oxygen travels from the air to the muscles in a chain: the lungs load it into
the blood, the heart pumps the blood, and the muscles extract the oxygen and use it. In a 2000 article,
David Bassett and Edward Howley set out why, in healthy people exercising with the whole body at sea
level, the limit sits mainly in delivery — the heart, lungs and blood — rather than in the muscles. [1]
When oxygen delivery is changed by blood doping, altitude or beta-blocker drugs, VO2 max changes with
it. When training raises VO2 max, it does so mainly by raising the heart's maximum output. And when only
one small muscle group works, so the heart can send it more of its output, that muscle can take up
two to three times as much oxygen as it manages during a whole-body effort, so the muscle's own
capacity is not what runs out first. [1] Endurance training can more than double the mitochondrial
enzymes in muscle, yet in the human studies they cite VO2 max rose far less, by 20 to 40 per cent. [1]

**What VO2 max does not decide.** It sets an upper limit on endurance performance, but it is not the
only thing that matters. [1] Two runners with the same VO2 max can differ in running economy (how
much oxygen a given pace costs) and in the share of their VO2 max they can sustain for a whole race.
Bassett and Howley conclude that running speed at the lactate threshold, which combines all three, is
the best physiological predictor of distance-running performance. [1] Their example: a 2:15
marathon needs an oxygen uptake of about 60 mL/kg/min held for the whole race, and since marathons
are run at roughly 80 to 85 per cent of VO2 max, it needs a VO2 max of about 70.5 to 75. [1] That is
also why our [race time prediction](/articles/race-time-prediction/) article treats a predicted time
as an extrapolation rather than a promise.

<h2 id="health">Why it matters for health</h2>

Fitness researchers usually express VO2 max in METs: one MET is the resting rate of oxygen use, about
3.5 mL/kg/min, so 10 METs is about 35 mL/kg/min. [3]

**The pooled evidence.** A 2009 meta-analysis by Satoru Kodama and colleagues combined 33 cohort
studies of healthy men and women — 102,980 people for the analysis of death from any cause. [4]
Each 1-MET higher fitness level, which the authors equate with running 1 km/h faster, was associated
with a 13 per cent lower risk of death from any cause and a 15 per cent lower risk of coronary heart
disease or cardiovascular events. The least fit, below about 7.9 METs, had a 70 per cent higher risk
of death than the fittest, at about 10.9 METs or above — cut-offs the authors set for a 50-year-old
man. [4]

**A large clinical cohort.** A 2018 study followed 122,007 adults who had a treadmill exercise test
at the Cleveland Clinic, a large academic medical centre, between 1991 and 2014, for a median of 8.4
years. [5] Fitness was estimated from the speed and slope each person reached on the treadmill and graded against
others of the same age and sex. Risk of death fell at every step up in fitness. The fittest group —
the top 2.3 per cent for their age and sex — had a fifth of the adjusted risk of the least fit, and
the study found no level above which the benefit stopped. The gap between below-average and
above-average fitness (a hazard ratio of 1.41) was as large as the gap associated with smoking (1.41)
or diabetes (1.40). [5]

**Where most of the difference lies.** A 2016 American Heart Association scientific statement, which
reviewed this evidence and argued that fitness should be assessed routinely as a clinical vital sign,
makes a point that is easy to miss. [3] Most of the difference is at the bottom. Fitness below 5 METs is
associated with high mortality risk, and more than half of the drop in deaths occurs between the least
fit group and the next one up. [3] You do not need to be athletic to gain most of what fitness offers.
The statement also reports that people whose fitness improved between two tests had lower risk than
people whose fitness did not: in one study of 9,777 men, those who went from unfit to fit had 44 per
cent lower mortality than those who stayed unfit. [3]

**What this evidence cannot show.** All of it is observational. The 2018 study's authors are explicit
that their data cannot show whether fitness lowers the risk of death or whether healthier people are
simply fitter, and that people referred for a treadmill test differ from the general population. [5]
The 2009 meta-analysis found signs of publication bias, though allowing for it only moderately weakened
its estimates. [4] About half the variation in fitness between people is thought to be inherited. [3]
The association is strong and consistent, but it is an association.

<div class="summary-body"><p>In large cohort studies, each 1-MET step up in fitness goes with roughly 13 per cent lower risk of death, and the largest difference lies between the least fit and the next group up. The evidence is observational, so it shows a strong link, not proof of cause.</p></div>
</aside>

<h2 id="measuring">Measuring it, and estimating it</h2>

**The measurement.** VO2 max is measured in a laboratory by analysing the air you breathe out during
a test that gets harder until you stop, and the American Heart Association calls a measured peak
the "gold standard" for exercise capacity. [3] Even in the lab, about half of people do not show a
plateau in oxygen uptake at the end of the test, so testers use other signs that the effort was
maximal. [1] Everything else is an estimate, and how good an estimate is depends on whom it was
built on.

**The 12-minute run.** In 1968, Kenneth Cooper had 115 US Air Force men run as far as they could in
12 minutes and then measured their VO2 max on a treadmill. [2] Distance and VO2 max were closely
related (a correlation of 0.897), and Cooper turned the relationship into an equation. The men were
young — an average age of 22, most under 25 — and Cooper wrote that it was difficult to predict the
test's accuracy in older people, though early signs up to age 50 were favourable, and that its
accuracy depends directly on how hard people try. [2] No women were tested.

**The heart-rate ratio.** A 2004 method by Niels Uth and colleagues estimates VO2 max as 15.3 times
your maximum heart rate divided by your resting heart rate. [6] It was developed and tested in 46
well-trained men aged 21 to 51. With a measured maximum heart rate, the standard error of the
estimate was 2.7 mL/kg/min. With a maximum predicted from age instead, it rose to 4.7, and the
range covering 95 per cent of individual errors widened to about 9.2 mL/kg/min either way — roughly 15 per cent. [6]
The authors warned that the factor of 15.3 may differ by age, sex and training level, and should be
checked before the method is used on anyone else. [6] They also measured resting heart rate lying in
bed on waking, because posture alone changes the numbers. [6]

**Watches.** A 2022 meta-analysis pooled 14 studies, with 403 participants in all, that compared
consumer wearables' VO2 max estimates with laboratory tests. [7] Devices that estimate from
measurements at rest overestimated VO2 max by 2.17 mL/kg/min on average, with individual errors
running from 13 below to 17 above. Devices that estimate from a run or other exercise were right on
average, but individual errors still ran about 10 mL/kg/min either way. [7]

The authors concluded that exercise-based estimates are good enough to describe groups of people but
not yet accurate enough for individuals, and that there is too little evidence on whether a watch
gives the same answer twice to trust it for tracking one person's changes. [7] Put that beside the
health research: changes of 1.75 to 3.5 mL/kg/min have been linked to lower risk, which is smaller
than these errors. [7] Most participants were young adults, 11 of the 14 studies were judged at high
risk of bias, and the work was partly funded by Huawei, a wearable maker. [7]

<h2 id="raising-it">How to raise it</h2>

**Intensity matters.** The trial behind the "4×4" interval session was published in 2007 by Jan
Helgerud and colleagues in Norway. [8] Forty moderately trained young men, with an
average age of 25, ran on a slightly inclined treadmill three times a week for eight weeks in one of
four ways, each set up to cost the same total amount of oxygen:

- 45 minutes of continuous running at 70 per cent of maximum heart rate
- about 24 minutes of continuous running at the lactate threshold, 85 per cent of maximum heart rate
- 47 repeats of 15 seconds at 90–95 per cent, each followed by 15 seconds at 70 per cent
- four 4-minute intervals at 90–95 per cent, each followed by 3 minutes of active recovery at 70 per cent

Only the two interval groups improved VO2 max: by 5.5 per cent for the 15-second intervals and 7.2
per cent for the 4-minute intervals, which took the 4×4 group from 55.5 to 60.4 mL/kg/min. [8] The
two continuous groups did not change. The interval groups' stroke volume, the amount of blood pumped
per heartbeat, rose by about 10 per cent, in step with the gains in VO2 max. Running economy
improved similarly in all four groups. [8] The authors recommended the longer intervals because
holding 90–95 per cent of maximum heart rate across 47 short repeats was difficult. [8] The full 4×4
session is a 10-minute warm-up, the four intervals with their recoveries, and a 3-minute cool-down
at 70 per cent. [8]

It is one eight-week trial in young men. A 2015 meta-analysis by Zoran Milanović and colleagues
pooled 28 controlled trials with 723 healthy adults aged 18 to 45, mostly young (an average age of
25). [9] Against no exercise, both continuous training and intervals raised VO2 max a lot: by 4.9 and
5.5 mL/kg/min. Where the two were compared directly, intervals came out ahead by 1.2 mL/kg/min, a
difference the authors rate as possibly small. [9] Their tests also suggested publication bias across
all the analyses. [9]

The American Heart Association's review points the same way. When total work is held constant, interval training is likely to
raise fitness more than moderate continuous training, though results across studies are
inconsistent. [3] The fitter you start, the harder you need to train: the statement suggests about
50 per cent of heart-rate reserve is enough below 10 METs, 65 to 85 per cent between 10 and 14 METs,
and over 85 per cent above that. [3]
Our [heart rate zones article](/articles/heart-rate-zones-explained/) explains heart-rate reserve. For
previously sedentary adults, exercise in line with guidelines typically raises fitness by about 10
per cent. [3]

**What changes in the body.** Training raises VO2 max mainly through a larger maximum stroke volume
and greater oxygen extraction by the trained muscles. [3] In a 2017 trial of six weeks of cycling in young
men, the rise in total haemoglobin mass — the blood's oxygen-carrying protein — was the strongest independent
predictor of the rise in VO2 max. [10]

**Why people respond differently.** In the HERITAGE Family Study, 481 sedentary white adults aged 17 to
65, from 98 families, cycled three times a week for 20 weeks, building up from 30 minutes at
the heart rate matching 55 per cent of their VO2 max to 50 minutes at 75 per cent. [11] The average gain was about
400 mL/min, but some people gained little or nothing while others gained more than 1,000 mL/min. The gain
ran in families: there was 2.5 times as much variation between families as within them, and the
authors estimated that up to 47 per cent of it was familial, a figure that includes shared
environment as well as genes. [11]

**Non-responders.** The idea that some people simply cannot improve was tested directly in a 2017
trial by David Montero and Carsten Lundby. [10] Seventy-eight healthy, untrained young men cycled for
six weeks in one of five groups doing one to five 60-minute sessions a week. The share whose maximal
power output did not improve beyond measurement error was 69, 40 and 29 per cent with one, two and
three sessions, and zero with four or five. Those non-responders then trained for six more weeks with
two extra sessions a week, and every one of them improved. [10] The men were young and chose their
own group rather than being randomly assigned, and non-response was judged by power output rather
than VO2 max, so the figures are not a general rate. But the direction is clear: in this trial, not
improving meant not training enough.

<div class="summary-body"><p>Continuous training and intervals both raise VO2 max, and in direct comparisons intervals do slightly better; in one trial matched for total work, only intervals did. Gains come mainly from the heart and blood. People respond very differently, partly through family, but in one trial every non-responder improved with more training.</p></div>

<h2 id="bottom-line">The bottom line</h2>

VO2 max is the ceiling on how much oxygen your body can use, set mostly by your heart and blood. It
caps endurance performance without deciding it, and in large studies it tracks with how long people
live, most strongly at the low end.

**If you want to know yours,** only a laboratory test measures it. A 12-minute run or a watch gives an
estimate, and for one person that estimate can be off by more than the changes that matter for
health.

**If you want to raise it,** train hard enough and often enough. Steady training and intervals both
work, and hard intervals, such as four
4-minute efforts at 90–95 per cent of maximum heart rate, raised it in a trial where the same work at
easier intensities did not. And if you are not improving, the evidence suggests doing more before
deciding your body cannot.
