Creatine has more published research behind it than any other sports supplement, and it
costs roughly a few cents per serving. That combination makes it unusual: most things with
this much evidence are expensive, and most things this cheap have no evidence at all.

It is also surrounded by more folklore than almost anything else in the supplement aisle —
loading protocols, timing windows, cycling schedules, and a persistent kidney rumour that
refuses to die. Most of that is unnecessary.

<h2 id="what-it-is">What creatine actually is</h2>

Creatine is a compound your body already makes from three amino acids, and which you also
get from meat and fish. Roughly 95% of it is stored in skeletal muscle, most of it bound
to a phosphate group as phosphocreatine. [1]

A typical omnivorous diet supplies one to two grams a day, and the body synthesises about
the same again. That is enough for normal function, but it leaves muscle stores only
partially filled — usually around 60 to 80% of capacity. Supplementation exists to close
that gap. [2]

<span class="summary-label">Summary</span>
<div class="summary-body"><p>Creatine is made in the body and eaten in meat and fish. Ordinary diets leave muscle stores well short of full, which is the gap supplementation closes.</p></div>
</aside>

<h2 id="how-it-works">How it works</h2>

Very short, very hard efforts run on ATP, and muscle holds only a few seconds' worth.
Phosphocreatine is the fastest way to regenerate it — donating a phosphate group to turn
spent ADP back into usable ATP almost immediately.

More stored phosphocreatine means that regeneration continues slightly longer before you
have to slow down. In practice this shows up as an extra repetition, a marginally faster
final sprint, or better recovery between hard sets.

This is also why the benefit shrinks as efforts lengthen. The phosphocreatine system is
largely irrelevant to an hour of running, and the effect is concentrated in efforts lasting
a handful of seconds.

It is worth not overstating that, though. The meta-analysis grouped tasks by duration and
found small but statistically significant effects in **all three** bands — including efforts
longer than 150 seconds, where the effect size was much the same as for short maximal work.
[3] So "creatine does nothing for endurance" is too strong. Any benefit there is likely
indirect, coming from better-quality interval sessions rather than from the phosphocreatine
system doing anything useful during a steady hour.

<aside class="summary">
<span class="summary-label">Summary</span>
<div class="summary-body"><p>Creatine speeds ATP regeneration during brief maximal efforts. The benefit is clearest for sprinting and lifting, and smaller — though not zero — for longer endurance work.</p></div>
</aside>

<h2 id="benefits">What the benefits actually are</h2>

### Strength and power

This is the best-supported outcome. Meta-analysis of resistance-training studies shows
consistently greater gains in strength and lean mass in supplemented groups compared with
placebo, across a wide range of training programmes. [3]

Worth being precise about the size, because "well supported" and "large" are different
claims. The meta-analysis pooling 100 studies reported effect sizes of roughly 0.17 for body
composition and 0.24 for short maximal efforts — statistically clear, and small by
convention. [3] It is a modest amplifier on training you are already doing, not a substitute
for the training.

### Lean mass

Some of the early increase is water drawn into muscle cells, which is why the scale often
moves within the first week or two. Over longer periods the additional lean tissue appears
to be genuine, mediated mostly by the extra training volume creatine allows.

### Everything else

Claims around cognition, ageing, and mood are being actively researched and some early
results are interesting. They are not yet at the level of confidence that supports the
performance claims, and should be treated as promising rather than established.

<aside class="summary">
<span class="summary-label">Summary</span>
<div class="summary-body"><p>Strength and power gains are well established. Lean-mass gains are real but partly water early on. Cognitive and longevity claims remain preliminary.</p></div>
</aside>

<h2 id="dosing">Dosing and timing</h2>

**Three to five grams per day is the whole protocol.** Take it daily, including rest days.
Muscle saturation depends on consistent intake over weeks, not on any individual dose.

A loading phase — roughly 20 g per day split across four servings for five to seven days —
fills stores in about a week rather than three to four. [2]
It is faster, not better. Both approaches reach the same saturation point, and loading is
the main cause of the stomach discomfort people report.

On timing: the evidence for a meaningful pre- versus post-workout difference is weak.
Take it whenever you will remember to take it every day, because adherence matters far
more than the clock.

On cycling: there is no need. Stores simply decline over about a month if you stop.

On form: monohydrate remains the reference standard. A buffered version marketed as
substantially more potent was tested head-to-head against monohydrate at both its
recommended dose and a monohydrate-equivalent dose, and produced no greater change in muscle
creatine content, body composition, strength or anaerobic capacity. [4] It costs
considerably more.

**In fairness, that study was funded by a creatine monohydrate manufacturer**, so it had an
interest in the answer it got. We flag the same thing about the safety review above, and the
standard should not move depending on whether a conflict happens to point the way we already
lean. What makes the conclusion credible is not the study alone but that the buffered form's
own marketing claims have never been supported by peer-reviewed work at all.

<aside class="summary">
<span class="summary-label">Summary</span>
<div class="summary-body"><p>Take 3–5 g of monohydrate daily, at any time, indefinitely. Loading is optional and only changes how quickly stores fill. Premium forms are not better.</p></div>
</aside>

<h2 id="safety">Safety and side effects</h2>

The kidney concern originated from a misreading of a marker rather than from observed harm.
Creatine supplementation raises serum creatinine, which is also the marker used to estimate
kidney function — so a routine blood panel can look alarming without anything being wrong.

Studies looking directly at renal function in healthy adults have not found damage at
standard doses. A meta-analysis pooling this question found exactly the pattern you would
expect from a marker artifact rather than an injury: creatinine and urea readings shifted
upward, and the authors nonetheless concluded that supplementation does not induce renal
damage at the amounts and durations studied. [5] The blood test moves; the kidney does not.

Two honest limits on that. Only six studies met the bar for pooled analysis, so it is a
thin evidence base by meta-analytic standards. And it speaks to healthy adults at ordinary
doses — not to people who already have reduced kidney function. The largest safety review concluded that supplementation up to 30 grams
a day for as long as five years is well tolerated in healthy people, across populations from
infants to the elderly. [1]

**One thing worth knowing about that review.** It is a position stand from the International
Society of Sports Nutrition, prepared at the request of a supplement-industry trade body, and
several of its authors declare funding or consulting relationships with companies that sell
creatine. That does not make it wrong — its conclusions line up with independent work,
including the renal-function research above [5] — but a safety claim resting on an
industry-adjacent review deserves to be read knowing that, and we would rather tell you than
have you find out.

That said, if you have existing
kidney disease, are pregnant, or take medication affecting renal function, this is a
conversation for your clinician rather than an internet article.

The genuinely common side effects are minor: some water retention early on, and
gastrointestinal discomfort that is mostly associated with large single doses. Splitting
the dose or dropping the loading phase usually resolves it.

One practical note: tell your doctor you take creatine before any blood work, so an
elevated creatinine reading is interpreted correctly.

<aside class="summary">
<span class="summary-label">Summary</span>
<div class="summary-body"><p>No kidney harm has been demonstrated in healthy adults at standard doses. The concern came from a lab marker, not from observed damage. Mention it before blood tests, and consult a clinician if you have existing kidney disease.</p></div>
</aside>

<h2 id="bottom-line">The bottom line</h2>

Creatine monohydrate is cheap, well-studied, and modestly effective for exactly what it
claims — short, hard efforts. Take three to five grams daily, buy the plain monohydrate,
skip the loading phase unless you are impatient, and ignore anything marketed as an upgrade.

It will not compensate for insufficient protein, an unstructured programme, or poor sleep.
Get those right first — then creatine is a small, reliable addition on top.
