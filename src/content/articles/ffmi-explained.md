BMI has one well-known failure mode: it treats all mass identically. A heavily muscled
athlete and someone carrying substantial excess fat can produce the same BMI, and the index
will label both the same way.

Fat-free mass index fixes exactly this, by doing the same math on lean mass alone.

<h2 id="what-it-is">What FFMI actually is</h2>

Take your bodyweight, subtract the fat, and divide the remainder by your height in metres
squared. That is FFMI. Structurally it is BMI with the fat stripped out, which means it
measures how much lean tissue you carry relative to your frame.

Because it needs lean mass, it needs a body-fat estimate first. This is the main practical
limitation — an FFMI derived from a wild guess at body fat is a wild guess at FFMI.

The **normalised** figure adjusts the result to a 1.8 metre reference height using the
correction the original study specified, because taller
people tend to produce slightly lower raw values for the same relative muscularity.
Normalising makes comparisons across heights more meaningful.

<span class="summary-label">Summary</span>
<div class="summary-body"><p>FFMI is lean mass divided by height squared. It requires a body-fat estimate as an input, and the normalised version adjusts to a 1.8 m reference height for fair comparison.</p></div>
</aside>

<h2 id="vs-bmi">Why it beats BMI for trained people</h2>

Consider two men of the same height and weight. One carries 12 percent body fat, the other
30. Their BMI is identical. Their FFMI differs by several points, and it is the FFMI that
reflects what is actually going on.

<figure data-chart="bmi-vs-ffmi"></figure>

This is the concrete answer to the familiar complaint that BMI misclassifies athletes.
The complaint is correct, and FFMI is the fix. It is worth being precise about the scope of
that claim, though: BMI remains a reasonable population-level screening tool, and the
misclassification problem mostly affects the tails — the notably muscular and the notably
sarcopenic. For a sedentary population average, BMI is not as useless as fitness culture
sometimes suggests.

<div class="pull">
BMI answers "how heavy are you for your height". FFMI answers "how much of you is muscle".
Only one of those is a training metric.
</div>

<h2 id="interpreting">Interpreting your value</h2>

The most widely used reference set covers 5,635 adults and shows how FFMI distributes across
age and sex — more informative than the informal bands circulating in fitness communities. [2]
Here are its percentiles for plain FFMI, not height-normalised: half of each group sits below the
50th, 5% below the 5th, and 5% above the 95th. [2]

**Men**

| Age | 5th | 25th | 50th | 75th | 95th |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 18–34 | 16.8 | 18.0 | 18.9 | 19.8 | 21.1 |
| 35–54 | 17.2 | 18.3 | 19.2 | 20.1 | 21.7 |
| 55–74 | 17.0 | 18.4 | 19.4 | 20.3 | 22.1 |
| 75 and over | 16.6 | 17.6 | 18.5 | 19.4 | 21.2 |

**Women**

| Age | 5th | 25th | 50th | 75th | 95th |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 18–34 | 13.8 | 14.7 | 15.4 | 16.2 | 17.6 |
| 35–54 | 14.4 | 15.3 | 15.9 | 16.7 | 18.0 |
| 55–74 | 14.1 | 15.4 | 16.2 | 17.4 | 19.0 |
| 75 and over | 12.9 | 14.7 | 15.9 | 17.0 | 18.7 |

Women's medians are 2.6 to 3.5 points below men's, depending on age. The informal bands you will see
elsewhere — "average" at 18 to 20 and so on — are men's figures we could not trace to a published
source, so our calculator uses these percentiles instead.

Know what that reference set is before leaning on it. The sample was Swiss and Caucasian,
selected non-randomly, and body composition was measured by bioelectrical impedance rather
than DXA — a method cross-validated against DXA in that study, but still an estimate built
on an estimate. Percentiles from it describe that population, not necessarily yours. [2]

Two caveats on the measure itself. FFMI does not distinguish muscle from other lean tissue such as bone, organs
and water, so it is a proxy rather than a direct muscle measurement. And it says nothing
about strength, distribution or health — a high FFMI is not automatically a good thing, nor
a low one automatically bad.

<span class="summary-label">Summary</span>
<div class="summary-body"><p>FFMI has no official categories. A Swiss reference set gives percentiles by age and sex — a median of 18.9 for men and 15.4 for women aged 18–34 — while the bands that circulate are informal and for men. It measures all lean tissue, not muscle specifically, and says nothing about strength or health.</p></div>
</aside>

<h2 id="the-ceiling">The "natural limit" claim</h2>

You will frequently see the claim that an FFMI of 25 is the natural ceiling, and that
anything above it implies pharmaceutical assistance. This traces to a single 1995 study
of 157 male athletes — 83 steroid users and 74 non-users — in which the normalised FFMI of
the non-users extended up to a limit of 25.0, while many users exceeded it and some passed
30. A separate group of twenty pre-steroid-era Mr. America winners averaged 25.4. [1]

It is worth being careful with this. The sample was small, drawn from a specific population,
and **the authors themselves described their findings as preliminary** and proposed FFMI as
an initial screening measure rather than a verdict. [1] They also noted that their non-user
sample included only six men above 20 percent body fat, making it hazardous to generalise
the ceiling to heavier individuals. Individual variation
in frame size, limb length and genetics is substantial, and the measurement error in the
body-fat estimate feeding your FFMI is itself several points.

Treat 25 as a rough population observation rather than a diagnostic threshold. Using it to
make accusations about specific individuals asks far more of the number than the underlying
evidence supports.

<aside class="summary">
<span class="summary-label">Summary</span>
<div class="summary-body"><p>The FFMI 25 "natural limit" comes from one small 1995 study and was never framed as a hard biological ceiling. Individual variation and measurement error both exceed what a firm threshold would require.</p></div>
</aside>

<h2 id="bottom-line">The bottom line</h2>

FFMI is the better metric if you train, because it measures the thing you are actually
trying to change. Calculate it, note it, and re-check it every few months alongside your
body-fat estimate.

Just remember it inherits every bit of error from that body-fat figure — and treat the
famous ceiling number as a loose observation rather than a verdict.
