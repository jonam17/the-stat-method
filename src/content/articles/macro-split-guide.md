Search for the optimal macro split and you will find people arguing about 40/30/30 against
50/25/25 with the conviction of theologians. Most of that argument is about a variable that
matters considerably less than the one nobody argues about, which is how many calories you
eat in total.

<h2 id="hierarchy">The hierarchy that actually matters</h2>

Think of nutrition decisions in descending order of impact.

**Calories** determine whether you gain or lose weight. Nothing further down the list
overrides this. Controlled feeding studies that hold calories and protein constant while
varying the carbohydrate-to-fat ratio consistently find similar changes in body weight and
body fat.

**Protein** is the one macronutrient with a target worth hitting precisely, because it
protects lean mass in a deficit and supports its gain in a surplus.

**The carbohydrate and fat balance** comes third. It influences training performance,
satiety and adherence — real things — but it is not what decides whether the scale moves.

**Meal timing and food order** come a distant fourth, and are where most people spend a
disproportionate amount of attention.

<span class="summary-label">Summary</span>
<div class="summary-body"><p>Calories decide weight change, protein decides what kind of tissue you keep, and the carbohydrate-to-fat balance affects how you feel while doing it. Get them right in that order.</p></div>
</aside>

<h2 id="protein-first">Set protein first</h2>

Protein is the anchor because it is the only macro where falling short has consequences you
cannot compensate for elsewhere. In a deficit, insufficient protein means a larger share of
lost weight comes from lean tissue. In a surplus, it limits how much of the gain is muscle.

Our calculator sets protein at 1.8 g per kilogram of bodyweight, inside the International
Society of Sports Nutrition's range of 1.4 to 2.0 for people who exercise. [5] When you are
cutting and supply a body-fat figure, it uses 2.3 g per kilogram of lean mass instead, the
low end of the 2.3 to 3.1 recommended for lean athletes in a deficit. [4] That range is set
per kilogram of lean mass, so two people at ninety kilograms, one at twelve percent body fat
and one at thirty-five, get different targets from it.

Protein also has the highest thermic effect of the three macros and tends to be the most
satiating per calorie, which makes a deficit easier to sustain. That is a secondary benefit,
but it is a real one.

<aside class="summary">
<span class="summary-label">Summary</span>
<div class="summary-body"><p>Protein is the only macro where a shortfall cannot be offset elsewhere. Set it first, in the unit its range was set in, and treat it as the fixed part of the plan.</p></div>
</aside>

<h2 id="fat-floor">Then a fat floor, not a fat target</h2>

Dietary fat is necessary for hormone production and the absorption of fat-soluble vitamins.
The useful concept is a floor rather than a target — a level below which you should not go,
with everything above that being preference.

The Institute of Medicine's acceptable range for adults is 20 to 35% of calories from fat. [6]
Our calculator uses 25% by default and never goes below 20%. You will often see a floor
of 0.8 g per kilogram "for hormonal health"; we could not trace that figure to a source. In
studies that cut fat from about 40% to 20% of calories, testosterone fell modestly, but for
people dieting hard the size of the deficit and how lean they get appear to matter more than
the share of fat. [4] The practical experience of very low-fat diets is that food becomes
unsatisfying quickly.

Above the floor, more fat is a legitimate choice. Some people find higher-fat meals more
satiating and prefer to spend their remaining calories there.

<div class="pull">
Fat has a minimum you should respect. Beyond that, the ratio is preference, not physiology.
</div>

<aside class="summary">
<span class="summary-label">Summary</span>
<div class="summary-body"><p>Treat fat as a floor, 20% of calories, rather than a precise target. Above that line, how much you eat is a preference question.</p></div>
</aside>

<h2 id="carbs">Carbohydrate takes the remainder</h2>

Whatever calories are left after protein and fat go to carbohydrate. This is not a
demotion — for most people who train, carbohydrate is where the majority of remaining
calories should sit, because it fuels the high-intensity work that drives adaptation.

It is also worth saying plainly that the split itself does far less than the internet
suggests. A meta-analysis of 32 controlled feeding studies, swapping carbohydrate for fat at
matched calories and matched protein, found no metabolic advantage to carbohydrate
restriction — if anything, energy expenditure and fat loss were marginally greater on the
lower-fat side, by an amount too small to plan around. [1] Once protein and total calories
are set, the remaining ratio is mostly a question of what you can train on and stick to.

Which is not the same as saying it makes no difference at all — the difference just shows up
somewhere other than metabolism. When 17 obese men were fed either a very-low-carbohydrate or
a medium-carbohydrate diet at the same protein intake and allowed to eat as much as they
wanted, they reported less hunger and ate less on the low-carbohydrate arm, and lost more
weight over four weeks as a result. [3]

Read together, the two findings say something more useful than either alone. Composition does
not change what a calorie does to you; it can change how many calories you end up eating.
That is an adherence effect, and adherence is the thing that actually decides outcomes — which
is why the right split is the one you will follow, not the one with the best theory attached.

Training hard on very low carbohydrate is possible, but performance in glycolytic work —
sets in the six-to-twenty rep range, intervals, repeated efforts — is where the cost shows
up first. If your sessions feel flat and your calories and sleep are adequate, carbohydrate
is the first thing to look at.

The converse is also true. Very low fat in order to maximise carbohydrate runs into the
floor discussed above. Both extremes have costs, which is why the sensible region is broad
and central rather than at either edge.

<span class="summary-label">Summary</span>
<div class="summary-body"><p>Carbohydrate fills the remaining calories and fuels high-intensity training. Flat sessions with adequate calories and sleep usually point here first.</p></div>
</aside>

<h2 id="bottom-line">The bottom line</h2>

Set protein from lean mass, put a floor under fat, and let carbohydrate take what is left.
Then stop optimising and start executing, because a split you follow for three months beats
a theoretically superior one you abandon in a fortnight.

If you want to adjust from there, do it based on evidence from your own training and
appetite rather than from an argument on the internet. The calculator lets you type any
split you like — including one that is over budget, which it will flag in red while still
showing you the numbers.
