# Key takeaways review

The box at the top of each article — its key takeaways and closing line — rewritten in a warmer,
plainer voice. **The rule: wording may change, claims may not.** Every takeaway restates something
the article's body already says, and the body carries the citation; no takeaway adds a claim. Each
row below names the section a takeaway restates, so it can be checked against it.

Voice: speak to the reader as "you"; lead with what to do; plain words over jargon ("rehearse the
lift", not "rehearsing the movement pattern"); keep the numbers that matter; one idea per point,
each a complete claim.

Where a takeaway said something its own article does not support, it was corrected to match the
body, and that is listed under **Corrections**. Where the body contradicted itself, the sentence
that disagreed with the rest of its article was corrected — listed separately, because it changes
reviewed body text.

### Training volume and Progressive overload

Rewritten for voice only; each point restates the takeaway it replaces, which were verified in
those articles' citation reviews (`docs/citation-reviews/`). The volume article's "detectable growth
from about four sets" and "a fifth of the time" and the overload article's "about one more rep" are
unchanged figures.

### Corrections

- **One-rep max — takeaway.** "Rep-max formulas are curve fits to population data, not laws."
  The article says most "were never derived from research" — they began as charts and tables and
  were extrapolated afterwards. The new takeaway says so.
- **RPE — takeaway.** "Novices systematically underestimate how many reps they have left." The
  article says a meta-analysis of 12 studies found **no overall effect of training experience**, that
  almost everyone under-predicts by about one rep, and that "novices are worse" is "plausible and
  unproven". The new takeaway says that.
- **Strength standards — takeaway.** "…nothing about your potential, your health, or how long you
  have trained." The article never mentions training age there; it says potential, health, work ethic
  and whether training is well designed. The new takeaway matches.

### Body corrections (reviewed text — check these)

- **One-rep max, "Five formulas, five answers".** "The common equations were each fitted to a
  different sample, with different lifts and different training populations" contradicted the next
  section's finding that most were never fitted to research data. Now: "come from different sources —
  most began as coaches' loading charts rather than studies, as the next section explains." The
  section summary changed to match ("comes from a different source").
- **RPE, summary of "How well can people actually judge it".** "Novices under-rate their remaining
  capacity" contradicted the section it summarises. Now: "Almost everyone under-rates their remaining
  capacity by about a rep."

### Corrections

- **3,500-calorie rule — takeaway.** "Straight-line predictions overshoot, often by several kilograms
  over a year." The article says several kilograms over a **six-month** cut. Now matches.
- **Heart rate zones — takeaway.** "Most endurance benefit comes from accumulating easy volume." The
  article says trained athletes do most of their *volume* easy, and that easy volume builds aerobic
  capacity at low fatigue cost — not that it supplies most of the benefit. Now restates that.
- **Heart rate zones — closing line.** "Your own perceived effort corrects it faster than any
  equation." Perceived effort is not discussed in the article. The new line uses the article's own
  bottom line: use zones mainly to keep easy days easy.

### Corrections

- **Tracking without a scale — takeaway.** "Untrained visual portion estimates are … often [wrong] by fifty

- **What should I weigh — takeaway and closing line.** "Derived for clinical drug dosing." The article says
  the formulas came from clinical practice: Devine's for drug dosing, Hamwi's for diabetic diet planning. Now
  matches.
- **Creatine — takeaways.** "Newer, pricier variants have not beaten it in trials" rested on one trial, now
  stated as one; "weight gain in the first weeks is intracellular water" now "partly water", as the article
